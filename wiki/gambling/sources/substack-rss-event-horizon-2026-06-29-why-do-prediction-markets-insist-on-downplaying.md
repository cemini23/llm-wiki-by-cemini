## Narrative

Prediction market evangelists have been trying to downplay the dominance of sports event contracts in trading volume pretty much since sports trading started in early 2025. Alternatively, you could argue they’ve been trying to talk up trading on “everything else.”

 I saw a couple of new examples in recent days.

   There’s also this from a larger   Washington Post    interview with Kalshi CEO Tarek Mansour :

   But people aren’t as interested in news-driven event contracts.

 I don’t think that’s right. I think the growth of the non-sports predictions on Kalshi is higher than that of the sports predictions.

  But that’s because it’s probably starting from nothing.

 That’s not quite right. I think the latest numbers I was looking at were basically at two-thirds, one-third now. It’s going to eat into the sports very quickly. I mean, it’s happening.

  Before we move on, I am a fan of the promise of “everything else” at prediction markets. I think there are a lot of markets that provide us with economic value, insight, and price discovery.

 Sports event contracts are not in that bucket of markets. And betting on “everything else” is certainly growing in raw dollar amounts, but not really as a share of notional volume.

 First, let’s break down the claims above:

    From a16z:   “The World Cup is attracting lots of interest and activity, and other areas are growing alongside. Non-sports volume — across categories like politics, economics, geopolitics, and current events — hit $3.6B last week on Kalshi and Polymarket combined. That’s larger than total prediction market volume (sports included) was just last year.”

   Is that true? I don’t know, because a16z doesn’t include the category map or volume methodology behind the chart. But for the week ending June 22, Kalshi had roughly $9 billion in volume, and Polymarket had about $3 billion via the international site, and $1 billion from the US app. We can disregard the US app, since 99% of volume there is sports.

   Kalshi is doing a little more than $1 billion in non-sports, non-parlay volume. Parlays, of course, are almost entirely sports, so to classify them as non-sports would not be a terribly serious way to approach the data. It also looks like Polymarket is doing a little less than a billion in non-sports volume internationally in recent weeks. (It’s also worth noting that the share of sports at Polymarket has actually gone up  considerably  with the World Cup.)

   So…I can’t get to their number without parlays, but parlays would also make the number way more than $3.6 billion. So… :shrug emoji:. If we use all Kalshi and Polymarket volume — including Polymarket US and International, but exclude parlays — I get to around 15% of volume is non-sports.

        WaPo with Mansour (one-third not sports):  I am not sure what his source is, nor do I know whether he is using prediction markets as a category or just Kalshi by itself. And it’s not clear he’s even talking about volume, but that’s generally the industry standard.

    If he’s going by a16z’s number of $3.6 billion in a week… yeah, we’re pretty close to that 2/3 vs. 1/3 split. But, like I said above, I have some questions about that number. (Also, as a quick aside: Kalshi considers Polymarket to be an illegal, offshore site, so I am not sure why they would lump together their data. But I digress).

   At Kalshi alone, it’s definitely not one-third non-sports volume. For a time before the World Cup, sports volume as a share of total volume was decreasing. But since the start of the soccer tournament, sports + combos/parlays is now again nearing 90% of total volume. Everything else is usually around 10-15% of volume, and more than 10% of that is usually cryptocurrency markets.

   Another quick aside: If we’re talking about taker side/retail trading, then yes, we may be getting closer to that two-thirds/one-third split. But that’s not the number that Kalshi usually talks about.

   Even if we took out parlays entirely, then we’re still only talking about 20% of Kalshi’s volume being non-sports.

   It’s worth noting: Cryptocurrency trading has really grown at Kalshi…it’s now a massive category there. Most of that is on Bitcoin, but it’s grown as a share of all volume and in raw dollars.

   The “everything else” beyond that remains almost a rounding error. Out of $10.8 billion in Kalshi volume in the trailing seven days, less than $200 million of that is on things other than sports, parlays, and crypto. And the next biggest category is mention markets, which I would argue are not serious/important markets either.

         I felt the need to break down the data, but that’s almost beside the point. The larger question I have is this:

 If sports event contracts are so   important in the world — and have utility in economic hedging and price discovery — why do people try to paint a picture that non-sports is growing? Aren’t sports events just important?

 Well, the answer might be obvious, but I think it’s worth saying it out loud. The longer we go on with prediction markets being dominated by sports volume, the more they get seen as glorified sports betting exchanges. If they are just sports betting with a little bit of other trading tacked on, that’s not really delivering on the promise or the “big idea” behind prediction markets, at least as they started out.

 And while true believers will tell you the prediction markets will win in the courts at the end of the day, that’s far from a guarantee. Kalshi and Polymarket both want and need to be prediction markets for the “everything else” in order to future-proof themselves.

 Anyway, I find it interesting that prediction-market companies argue that sports event contracts are legal and socially useful, while also trying to convince everyone that they are shrinking as a share of trading.

 It’s still sports. A lot of sports. That’s what the data tells us.

 Will non-sports markets catch up in the US? That’s the aspiration. But it’s not entirely clear how that happens.
