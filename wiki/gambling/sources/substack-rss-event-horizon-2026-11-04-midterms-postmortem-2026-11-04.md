The follow-up to the pre-election piece filed on 2026-11-03. That one argued both outcomes were bad for the industry; this one records which happened.

## Narrative

### The result

**Democrats won both chambers.** Polls and prediction markets both anticipated a strong Democratic night. **The markets got the direction right.**

The author's own caveat is the important part: they "might not have captured the breadth or intensity" of it.

### The contract structure tells you why

This is the concrete evidence behind that caveat. Kalshi's **"blue wave"** contract resolved Yes on:

> Democrats hold **218 or more** House seats AND **49 or more** Senate seats.

A separate **"megatsunami"** contract required **240+ House and 54+ Senate** — and was *not* favoured.

So the market priced a threshold far below the actual outcome. When Democrats won Senate seats and governorships in places Republicans were decent favourites — South Carolina Senate, Texas governor — and margins blew past forecasts, the market was right on the binary and wrong on the scale.

**That is a structural property of threshold contracts, not a forecasting failure.** A market that asks "will X exceed 218" cannot express "X will be 240". Directional accuracy and magnitude accuracy are different products, and the contract only sold one.

### Markets faster than media

Prediction markets converged on winners and control of Congress **materially earlier than media outlets called them**. The author notes the same happened in 2024.

That is the strongest evidence in the piece for the market's comparative advantage — not accuracy against polls, but **speed against decision desks**. It is also the claim least vulnerable to the argument that markets are just polls with extra steps.

### The reversal the pre-election piece predicted

The 2026-11-03 note argued both outcomes hurt: a bad call would cost credibility, a Democratic win would cost congressional goodwill. The author now confirms the second: "Congressional trouble on the horizon for prediction markets?"

**The industry got the roundabout outcome it did not want** — vindicated on accuracy and worse off politically.

### The honest summary

The author declines to over-read it: "prediction markets saw Democrats winning. And Democrats won. For now, we don't need to overthink it."

That restraint is right. One election, where markets and polls agreed, cannot separate market skill from poll skill. The differentiator the piece does demonstrate is timing, and timing is measurable.

**Caveat.** Single source, written Tuesday night from Kalshi data only, before results were final.
