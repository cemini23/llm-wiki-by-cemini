## Narrative

Last week, I wrote about the proposed budget in North Carolina that would establish prediction markets as legal in the state with a very small tax and no regulation:

     The Closing Line    How Did Prediction Markets Get This Sweetheart Deal In The North Carolina Budget?   Editor’s note: There is no gambling news roundup in this newsletter…   Read more   6 days ago · 12 likes · 2 comments · Dustin Gouker    The legislature has now passed the budget, and it’s just  awaiting the signature of Gov. Josh Stein .

 I am sending this newsletter to you mostly so that you will read  this piece from NC Insider at State Affairs , which outlines how silly the “process” on this prediction markets policy is. (It appears anyone can get access if you sign up for free.)

 There is a ton of fascinating/disturbing insight from the state legislature (emphasis added):

  Top budget writer Sen. Brent Jackson, R-Sampson, said he first saw the language of the prediction market provision a day before the budget was published. He said it was included at the behest of House lawmakers.

 Jackson acknowledged he didn’t have much knowledge on prediction markets, but added he didn’t foresee the state’s de facto recognition of the activity cutting into the state’s sporting betting operations.  He said sports betting often involves individuals pulling for a team they’re already a fan of, while prediction markets offer more niche betting options.

  Excuse me? What on earth are you talking about? That’s not at all how any of this works. I wanted to toss my laptop across the room when I read this.

 In any event, the story paints a picture of provisions that were jammed into the budget at the last minute, with very little understanding of what was happening. There’s more where the passage above came from, so click on the story and support good journalism.

 Will sportsbooks quit North Carolina to offer prediction markets? Probably not. But maybe someday! Will prediction markets start impacting revenue from the state’s sports betting industry if they are actively encouraged? Eventually!

 In any event, if you are going to formally legalize prediction markets at the state level, I think you owe your state a more serious discussion than what happened in North Carolina. Which apparently amounted to almost no discussion at all.

 I am also harping on this issue because it’s bigger than North Carolina. This is clearly a template upon which the prediction markets industry would like to build. If you want prediction markets in your state, by all means do something like this. I just think it requires more thought on policy than what we saw here.
