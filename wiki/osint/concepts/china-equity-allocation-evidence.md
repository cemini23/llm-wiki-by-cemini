## Raw Concept

Question this page answers: which arguments for a larger allocation to a national equity market survive evidence, using Chinese A-shares as the test case?

From Klement on Investing, "Should you have more China in your portfolio?" (2026-11-26), reviewing a Research Affiliates note by Hsu and colleagues.

## Narrative

### The five arguments

| Argument | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| The economy is large | Fails | Index weight and GDP level are unrelated |
| Growth will be higher | Fails | No correlation between GDP growth and stock returns |
| Volatility earns a premium | Fails | No evidence of a higher risk premium in A-shares |
| The index weight will rise | Unmeasurable | Inflow is estimable; the causal return share is not |
| Retail dominance creates alpha | Fails | No study links retail share to manager alpha |
| Correlation is low | Survives | About 0.4 with developed markets, stable in crises |

The first five are the common pitch. Only the last one is an allocation argument.

### Why the growth argument fails

Higher national GDP growth should raise earnings growth and therefore returns. Ritter and Klement have tested the link directly and found none. The reasons are structural: growth often comes from capital investment and new firm entry rather than per-share earnings, new listings dilute, and the equity index is not the economy.

This is a general result, not a China result. It rejects "buy the fast grower" for any country.

### Why the diversification argument survives

Chinese A-shares correlate about 0.4 with US and developed markets, and the correlation does not rise in crises. That is unusual. Most risky assets converge toward correlation one in a selloff, which removes the diversification exactly when it is needed.

Two conditions make it usable:

- **The low correlation must be measured in the crisis window**, not only across the full sample.
- **Volatility must be high enough that the return per unit of added volatility is favourable.** Chinese stocks are much more volatile than developed markets, and the note finds that return rises faster than volatility, up to a cap.

### The index-inclusion angle

MSCI includes A-shares at 20 percent of free float. Lifting that to full float would bring an estimated $79bn from AC World trackers and $240bn from EM trackers. A-share returns after the 2000 inclusion were 24.6 percent a year for five years, which is consistent with index buying pressure but does not prove it.

The correct handling of this argument is a probability-weighted trade, not a return forecast. The event is a policy decision; the payoff is an inflow estimate; neither is a valuation.

### The accounting-quality caveat

The note's most important exhibit is a distribution of return on equity. US data shows a small spike at zero ROE before 2002, the signature of small losses being managed into small profits, and the spike disappears after stricter accounting rules. Chinese distributions show a much larger anomaly.

That is a data-quality claim, not a return claim, and it should be tagged `[TENTATIVE]` until the underlying paper is read. If it holds, the practical rule is: valuation multiples from a market with weak accounting enforcement are not comparable to multiples from a strict one.

### Transferable rule

For any national market, rank the arguments in this order:

1. Correlation, measured in a crisis.
2. Return per unit of added volatility.
3. Valuation on comparable accounting.
4. Everything else — size, growth, retail structure — is narrative.

### Read

Use this page when a research note argues for a country weight. The five-argument table is the checklist.
