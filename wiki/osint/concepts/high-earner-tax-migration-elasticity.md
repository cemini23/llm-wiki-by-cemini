## Raw Concept

Question this page answers: when a country raises taxes on high earners, how much revenue does it gain, and how much does it lose to exit?

Built from Klement's summary of Giarola et al. (2026) on the Netherlands' 2012 reform of the 30% ruling.

## Narrative

**The reform.** The 30% ruling gave skilled foreign workers a maximum 30% income tax rate for up to ten years. The 2012 change added a wage threshold and removed eligibility within 150 km of the border. About 55,000 people used the scheme that year.

**The elasticity is concentrated at the top.** Users below the 90% income threshold stayed and paid the higher rate. The top 5% and top 1% earners were about 10% less likely to stay.

**The arithmetic.**

- Revenue lost from leavers: about €516.9m over five years.
- Extra revenue from stayers: about €497.3m over five years.
- Net: about €1.6m average annual loss.

Against 2019 income-tax receipts of almost €67bn, the authors call the reform cost-neutral. [TENTATIVE: one secondary summary of one study]

**The general point.** A tax rise aimed at the top trades two effects: higher receipts from those who stay, and exit by the most mobile. The second effect can cancel the first. The size of the effect depends on how mobile the group is, not on the size of the rate rise alone.

**Operator relevance.** Indirect. It is a prior for reading fiscal policy as a market factor: a headline rate rise is not a revenue estimate. Klement also cites Chris Rokos moving to Greece with about £330m of annual UK tax at stake [TENTATIVE].

**Caution.** The Dutch scheme applied to a specific expatriate population, so the result should not be generalised to a broad wealth tax without new evidence.
