## Raw Concept

Question this page answers: what does the option surface tell you that a single stock price does not?

Method from Moontower Meta (2026-11-15), which restates an argument by Jeff Yass.

## Narrative

A stock price is one number: the market's probability-weighted expected value. It cannot show spread. An option surface — every strike and expiry — shows the distribution the market is assigning.

Two consequences follow.

First, a high expected value and a likely bad outcome are compatible. When a technology shock opens many possible futures, the expected value can be high while the single most likely path is a loss. Prices falling is then uncertainty resolving, not a belief reversing.

Second, expensive far-out-of-the-money calls are not a forecast. In the natural-gas "widowmaker" spread the upside is unbounded, yet most probability mass sits near $3-4. Extreme calls are priced for a tail that is unlikely, and butterflies that would pay at those strikes price near zero.

The usable heuristic: read the surface for the shape, not the single price for the direction. A premium at an extreme strike prices a probability, not an expectation.

[TENTATIVE] One author's synthesis. The natural-gas example is illustrative, not a measured distribution.
