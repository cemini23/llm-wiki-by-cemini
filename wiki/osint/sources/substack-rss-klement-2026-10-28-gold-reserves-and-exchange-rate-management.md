## Narrative

Klement on Investing reviews the claim that central banks should hold more gold. One stated reason is protection when the Fed changes monetary policy in an unexpected way. The note says that kind of change looks more likely under the new Fed Chair. Gold, on this claim, would guard against currency devaluation and dollar devaluation. The note gives that view as background. It then tests a narrower question with one paper.

The note summarises Aizenman et al. (2026) on emerging-market central banks that try to hold the exchange rate steady against the dollar. The stated mechanism is reserve sales. A central bank with larger foreign-currency reserves can sell those reserves and buy its own currency. The aim is to slow depreciation against the dollar.

The note reads charts of the exchange-rate response to a 10bps surprise rate hike by the Fed. It says a one standard deviation higher stock of FX reserves (roughly 20 percentage points higher) lines up with 0.04 percentage points less exchange rate depreciation for every 10bps of Fed rate hikes. It says a one standard deviation higher stock of US dollar reserves reduces devaluation by 0.1 percentage points. It says non-dollar reserves have a much smaller impact, and that impact is statistically insignificant. These figures are single-source claims from this newsletter. This page does not check Aizenman et al. (2026).

The note treats gold as an indirect dollar asset because gold trades in US dollars. A second chart split compares all FX reserves and gold, FX reserves only, and gold only. The note says the dampening effect of gold reserves is similar in size to FX reserves in general. It says the uncertainty around the gold effect is higher, so the gold effect is statistically not significant. That comparison is also a single-source claim from this newsletter.

The note offers two soft conclusions. FX markets count gold like an indirect dollar asset that a central bank can sell for dollar liquidity. Gold reserves do not raise FX stability relative to holding dollars directly. For a quant-finance and macro reader, the split that matters is dollar reserves, non-dollar reserves, and gold. In this summary, only the dollar-reserve channel is statistically clear.
