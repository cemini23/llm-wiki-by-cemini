## Narrative

Klement reviews a lab experiment on how companies should select managers. A team at the London School of Economics, around **Ben Weidmann**, ran the study. They recruited **555 participants** and measured traits such as the **Big Five** personality traits and intelligence. They formed teams of **12 to 18 people** and promoted one member to manager. The teams then worked on group exercises that needed numerical, spatial, and analytical reasoning.

The test: in half the teams, a lottery chose the manager at random. In the other half, the person who said they were eager to become a manager, and thought they were a good one, got the job (self-selection).

Result: **randomly chosen managers performed better** than self-selected "careerists". The article then compares other criteria. The **Peter Principle** works to an extent: it beats random choice and matches emotionally intelligent managers. But two criteria work much better: promoting the **highest-IQ** people, and, best of all, selecting on **economic decision-making ability**.

Klement stresses what that means: not commercial thinking or chasing revenue, but the ability to allocate limited resources in the most efficient way, and to delegate each task to the person best able to do it. The source study is Weidmann et al. (2026), with a related study by Andrew Caplin and others.

Workspace relevance: behavioural and management research. It informs team and agent-selection thinking, but has no direct market link. Likely wiki-only Context; no distribution.
