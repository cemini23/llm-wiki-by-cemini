## Narrative

The post is an essay on when to act in a zero-sum game. It has no dataset. Its claims are about timing, not magnitude.

**The setup.** A friend built an HFT startup and declined a large job to do it. The firm raised once, from VCs rather than allocators, and is now self-financing. The author reads that as evidence, not anecdote. A manager with a mathematically visible edge does not need outside money. A manager with a long-horizon edge cannot demonstrate it with statistics. So the managers who want your money are, on average, the ones who cannot prove they have an edge. The author frames active-management fees as partly a lemons problem and partly spectacle.

**The 2×2.** The post leans on a right/wrong against consensus/contrarian grid. It says the grid is useful because it is proscriptive: an idea that is obviously right is consensus, and therefore already overbid.

**The Wolfe observation.** A Josh Wolfe interview from about a decade earlier is cited. Every giant winner in his fund was highly contentious at first, usually with one passionate champion on the investment committee. The post immediately adds the conditional: being contentious does not predict a winner. The statement only runs in the other direction, from a known winner back to its origin.

**The rule.** The post states the transferable behaviour: the ability to act before all the information is obvious. It says that if you need the evidence to be convincing, you are too late, and at that point the opposite action may be correct. It contrasts this with ordinary life, where waiting for more information usually improves the decision and carries no penalty.

The post extends the rule outside markets. It says political instinct and the trader's ability to flip a position quickly come from the same source. It cites Stan Druckenmiller flipping from long to short faster than any formal research process could run.

**The venture framing.** VC is described as a structural answer to the impossibility of forecasting. You cannot analyse which bet pays off, so you build a machine that attracts founders and diversify the tickets. Provided the sample has no mean, survival is most of the outcome. The post adds that survival has surprisingly little to do with returns.

**The related link.** Abraham Thomas's *Efficient Venture Markets Hypothesis* is recommended. The post's summary is that venture has become part of the market portfolio in the CAPM sense, and is now beta rather than a zero-sum contest.

## Dead Ends

- **Turning "act before the evidence is obvious" into a backtest rule.** The claim is about a decision that has no measurable signal at the time it is made. It cannot be tested in-sample. Any test would need a definition of "obvious" that the post does not supply.
