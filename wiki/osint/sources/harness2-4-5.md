## Narrative

Single-author STS / conceptual-analysis paper by Andrew D. Maynard (ASU). Argument per the preview: in early 2026 the AI field rapidly consolidated on the term "harness" to describe the software infrastructure around LLMs (tools, memory, prompts, guardrails, orchestration — what turns a raw model into a working agent). Maynard argues (preview cuts off mid-sentence) that while the engineering practices the metaphor describes are real, the metaphor itself shapes how we conceptualize the human–AI relationship.

Honest assessment: this is critical-theory / STS commentary on AI engineering, not an engineering paper. 12 pages, single-author, dated March 5 2026 — a timely response to the harness-terminology convergence. Value for a technical reader is the meta-awareness: what the word "harness" implies (control, yoking, harnessing a work animal) vs. what the practices actually do. The companion preprint harness-4-5 (Jiun Kim) proposes harness engineering as a discipline; this paper critiques the metaphor underlying that discipline's name.

Relevance to Cemini: indirect. Useful if the user wants to think clearly about the metaphors shaping their own architectural choices, but contains no direct technical guidance.

## Key findings

- Documents the rapid mid-decade convergence on "harness" as the canonical term for LLM-surrounding software (tools, memory, prompts, guardrails, orchestration).
- Argues that while the engineering practices are real, the metaphor shapes conceptualization of human–AI relations.
- Short (12p), single-author, STS/conceptual analysis genre — not empirical.
- Companion to harness-4-5 (Jiun Kim) which takes the opposite angle: proposing harness engineering as a discipline.
