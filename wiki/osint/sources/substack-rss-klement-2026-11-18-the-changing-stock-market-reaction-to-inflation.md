## Narrative

Klement reports a Bank of Italy study by D'Andrea et al. (2025) on how US and European stocks react to inflation surprises.

Before 2020 the textbook result held. A positive inflation surprise was negative for the share price, and more negative for firms with more long-dated debt relative to assets. Higher inflation raises costs, raises the discount rate, and hurts levered firms most. [Source: substack-rss-klement-2026-11-18-the-changing-stock-market-reaction-to-inflation.md]

Since 2020 the sign flipped. A positive inflation surprise is positive for the share price, and more positive for highly levered firms.

The proposed mechanism is the type of shock. In the 2000s and 2010s inflation shocks were mostly demand-driven, so a central bank could cool them by hiking. Since the pandemic they have been supply-driven, as in the 1970s. A central bank cannot move tankers through the Strait of Hormuz. A persistent supply shock devalues nominal debt in real terms, which helps a levered balance sheet and the share price. If the shock becomes too large, profits suffer and the effect reverses, as in 2023. [Source: substack-rss-klement-2026-11-18-the-changing-stock-market-reaction-to-inflation.md]

The usable rule: the sign of the equity reaction to an inflation surprise depends on the shock's origin. A leverage tilt is only correct under a supply-shock regime. Tag `[TENTATIVE]`; one secondary summary of one study.
