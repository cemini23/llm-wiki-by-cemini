## Narrative

A concise framework paper proposing AI governance as the structured response to the ethical, legal, and societal implications of deploying AI. Ligot first sets context: two AI system types — discriminative AI (prediction, classification, pattern recognition) and generative AI (novel content generation) — and the two architectures driving the current wave — transformers (LLMs) and latent diffusion models (image generation). He identifies four engineering challenges (training-data quality, prompt engineering, misuse prevention, acceptable-use guidelines) and four governance roles (builders, users, planners, trainers). Five ethical considerations are emphasized — accuracy, human oversight, transparency, accountability, fairness — and the paper catalogs 14 widely-circulated generative-AI risks (misinformation/disinformation, IP infringement, bias and discrimination, privacy violations, impersonation/identity fraud, malicious code generation, deepfake exploitation, automated spam/phishing, harmful content, job displacement, academic-integrity erosion, climate impact, digital-divide widening, and misaligned AI systems).

The paper's contribution is the **4E Framework** — a holistic model for effective AI governance: **Education** (equipping specialists and the public to navigate AI responsibly), **Engineering** (aligning systems via RLHF and drift prevention), **Enforcement** (incentives for responsible R&D, acceptable-use standards, penalties for misuse), and **Ethics** (risk management, regulatory compliance, value alignment). Ligot argues that frameworks like the 4E model are pivotal for building trust, transparency, and accountability across AI development, deployment, and use.

**Cemini relevance**: a compact, citable governance scaffold — the 4E Framework and the 14-risk catalog give Cemini a ready checklist for self-assessment ahead of institutional-acquirer due diligence, and its risk taxonomy complements the harder legal typology in Sylvia Lu's *Regulating AI Harms* (companion source ai-6-4-6).
