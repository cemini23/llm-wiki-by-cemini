## Narrative

### What it says

Klement reviews a Research Affiliates note by Jason Hsu and colleagues on whether Chinese A-shares deserve a larger index weight. A-shares are about 0.4 percent of MSCI AC World and about 4 percent of MSCI Emerging Markets, and MSCI includes them at only 20 percent of free-float market cap to reflect liquidity constraints. [Source: substack-rss-klement-2026-11-26-should-you-have-more-china-in-your-portfolio.md]

He works through five arguments for a higher weight and gives a verdict on each. The page @concepts/china-equity-allocation-evidence.md holds the general result.

### The numbers that matter

| Claim | Number |
|---|---|
| A-share weight in MSCI AC World | about 0.4% |
| A-share weight in MSCI EM | about 4% |
| MSCI inclusion factor for A-shares | 20% of free float |
| GDP-weighted portfolio return, May 2005 to March 2020 | 6.3% p.a. |
| MSCI AC World return, same window | 6.1% p.a. |
| Correlation of A-shares with US and developed markets | about 0.4 |
| Potential inflow if the 20% factor is lifted | $79bn from AC World, $240bn from EM |
| A-share return in the five years after 2000 inclusion | 24.6% p.a. |

### The argument that survives

Only one argument holds up: diversification. The 0.4 correlation with developed markets is low, it stays low in crises, and correlations are high enough between Chinese stocks that adding them lifts return faster than volatility, up to a point.

Everything else fails:

- **Size.** China is the second-largest economy. GDP level does not drive equity returns.
- **Growth.** Klement and Jay Ritter have tested GDP growth against stock returns and found no correlation.
- **Risk premium.** Higher A-share volatility is not matched by evidence of a higher risk premium.
- **Front-running the index.** The inflow estimate is real, but how much of the 2000 rerating came from index buying is unknown.
- **Retail dominance.** No study links retail share to manager alpha.

### The part that is a warning, not an argument

The post's core exhibit compares the distribution of return on equity for US and Chinese listed companies. US stocks show a small jump at zero ROE from 1984 to 2001, a classic sign of earnings management, and the jump disappears after the post-2000 accounting cleanup. The Chinese distributions show a pattern that Klement calls systematic and widespread earnings manipulation.

His own conclusion: he would not buy A-shares at any index weight.

### Claim status

- The US zero-ROE jump and its post-2000 disappearance: `[CONFIRMED]` — a well-documented accounting-cleanup effect.
- The Chinese earnings-management reading: `[TENTATIVE]` — one working paper (Wool et al. 2026) and one author's interpretation. The chart is not reproduced here.
- The index weights and correlations: `[TENTATIVE]` — single secondary summary.

### Read

The transferable lesson is not "avoid China". It is that a country's share of world GDP is not an allocation argument, and that a low correlation is an allocation argument only if it survives a crisis. Both are testable for any market.
