## Narrative

Kyla's Newsletter published this post on 2026-10-29. The main item is an interview with Lael Brainard, former Vice Chair of the Federal Reserve and former Director of the National Economic Council. She now chairs the Kitchen Table Project. The project holds listening sessions in 12 communities, runs polls, and turns the results into proposals. The same post launches Kyla's show Best of Luck, with Kai Ryssdal as episode one, and corrects a Raymond Williams quotation. The grocery interview is the macro material.

Kyla says affordability is "polling in a tier by itself" ahead of the election. She attributes that line to one pollster in the Washington Post. She cites a Post analysis of 46 House districts that will decide control of Congress. In 42 of those districts, incomes did not keep up with costs from 2019 to 2024. Median costs rose 33% and median incomes rose 28%. Six in ten households in those districts cannot cover a basic family budget. This file states those Post figures as Kyla's citation.

Brainard says people name groceries more often than housing or healthcare as a cause of economic distress. She says 99% of Americans shop for groceries once a month and 75% shop weekly. Kyla's lead says nearly every American shops at least monthly, three-quarters shop weekly, and two-thirds call grocery prices unfair. Both say 83% blame tariffs and 73% blame the war. Kyla names the war in Iran. Brainard links the war to high gas prices, high diesel prices, and higher food-packaging costs. Kyla says six in ten shoppers use more than one store, and seven in ten parents do. Brainard says a large majority drive from store to store, and later says three-quarters shop that way. The post gives two readings of tariff pass-through. Kyla says one in nine people (11%) see better prices after some food tariffs came off, and 42% think prices got worse. In the interview, Brainard says 10% see lower store prices and 40% think prices got worse. She says banana and coffee prices have not come down with the tariffs. She says retailers raise prices when costs rise and then resist cuts when tariffs fall.

The project states three proposals. Each figure is an annual saving for a family of four. This newsletter is the only source in the file for each figure.

- End tariffs on food and food inputs, and make the saving reach the shelf. The lead says over $320 a year. Brainard says grocery prices rose by about $320 a year because of the tariffs, and that families could save $320 over a year if a task force checks pass-through. The task force would use USDA price monitoring, FTC investigations, and a consumer complaint portal. Brainard says this uses tools the government already has and adds no budget cost.
- Require large unit prices and mandatory shrinkflation labels. The lead says over $460 a year. Brainard says $460 per family per year. She says the estimate is about a 3% saving. She says behavioral research finds savings of 5% to 15%. She says survey support is close to 90%. She also says honest prices, in the future, means no surveillance pricing, such as a higher price when a parent buys baby medicine at midnight.
- An Affordable Groceries Partnership. The lead says over $315 a year. USDA and the Department of Defense already buy staples for school lunches, veterans, and military bases. Participating private grocers could buy on those wholesale contracts and pass the saving through on a staple basket. They would keep only enough margin to cover their cost. Brainard says there is no subsidy and no new fiscal cost. She says taxes have gone down and deficits are larger, so the project looks for relief with no fiscal cost. She says national chains already hold purchasing power, smaller grocers and rural grocers do not, and the public cannot see when chains keep the margin. The basket is existing staples such as milk, eggs, flour, cereal, canned food, frozen food, and fresh food. Kyla compares the plan to a public-sector store brand.

Brainard stacks the three items at about $1,000 a year for a family of four, on a grocery basket that may cost about $15,000 a year. She also says tariff removal plus honest prices is already "well in excess of $700", and that consumers would see "over $750" within months. She says all three items reach "well above $1,000". Kyla says respondents wanted to see $500 in savings for the policies to count. Kyla's close says the package comes to about $1,000 a year. She supports honest pricing and opposes broad tariffs on food. She says she does not know whether grocers will join the partnership.

Kyla says consumer sentiment is near all-time lows while people still spend. Brainard says the spending continues, and that search time, smaller packages, and a sense of dishonest prices leave people angry. She calls a demand for a full drop in the price level "affordability doomerism", and she says that drop arrives in recessions. She says people ask essentials to rise more slowly, and a large majority in the survey want savings now. Kyla says 77% of shoppers worry about food safety and foodborne illness. Brainard cites cuts at the Department of Agriculture under DOGE, including food-safety inspectors, and cites the new world screwworm in cattle herds and imports. Kyla quotes Gallup: 43% of U.S. adults say the federal government is effective at a positive impact on people's lives, down eight points from 51% in 2025. The same note says 92% say the federal government has some or a great deal of power to make a positive impact, against 93% for state and local government and 88% for businesses. Kyla reads the survey through Albert Hirschman's Exit, Voice, and Loyalty (1970). Multi-store trips and generic brands are exit. The complaint portal is voice. Unit prices and shrinkflation labels make the exit informed. The partnership is another exit option.

For a quant-finance and macro research reader, the post separates three grocery-price channels: tariff pass-through, package size, and access to public wholesale contracts. It also separates spending from sentiment, and it names search costs (extra stores, gas, and time) that sit outside a shelf-price index. The dollar savings, the poll shares, and the $15,000 basket are Kitchen Table Project figures in this single newsletter. The file states no method, no sample size, and no official series id for them. The 33% cost figure and the 28% income figure are a Washington Post citation inside the newsletter. The Gallup shares are a quotation inside the newsletter.
