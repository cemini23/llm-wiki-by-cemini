## Narrative

Italy has a steep north–south gradient in economic prosperity. The common explanation is trade access. The north traded with Europe, the Middle East, and Asia, and the south did not. The usual date for the start is the rise of Venice as a trading hub in the 1300s.

Carla Salvo and Jacob Weisdorf (2026) push the start back about 300 years. The evidence is **church construction**. A prosperous community builds large buildings. So the authors measure the number and size of churches per square kilometre across Italy.

From about the year 1000, church floor area per unit area rises much faster in the north and centre than in the south and islands. The authors read this as a north-side money advantage.

The north's advantage drops twice in the Middle Ages, around **1300** and around **1500**. Both drops fit plague arrivals. An open trading economy imports goods and also imports pandemics. [Source: substack-rss-klement-2026-11-06-the-economic-divide-in-italy.md]

The conclusion is the standard one. Trade raises long-run prosperity. It also raises exposure to shocks. In the long run, trade wins, and the regions that stayed closed grew less for centuries.

### Why it matters here

Two limited uses.

1. **A natural experiment on openness.** The paper is a clean case for the trade-and-growth thesis with a long panel and a physical proxy for income.
2. **A caution on proxy quality.** Church construction is a coarse proxy. It measures a surplus that someone chose to spend on a durable public building. It measures that surplus relative to a neighbouring region, not in currency.

The article is a Friday "quirky economics" post. The last line, "Not that this would be relevant to any current developments," is a joke about the north–south gap in modern Europe.

## Dead Ends

- **Using church counts as an income series.** It is an ordinal proxy for surplus, not a level of output.
- **Routing this to Atto.** The topic is regional economic history, not Italian civil records or archival genealogy.
