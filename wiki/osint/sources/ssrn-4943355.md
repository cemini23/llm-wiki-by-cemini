## Narrative

This paper empirically examines how climate vulnerability affects sovereign fiscal risk, and whether political stability (specifically religious tensions) and financial development modulate that relationship. Panel data from ~100 countries, 1995-2019.

### Core findings

**Climate vulnerability → fiscal risk**: In highly climate-vulnerable economies, increased vulnerability leads to:
- **+0.5 to +1.5 percentage points** on government bond yields over two years [TENTATIVE]
- **Up to -1 notch** on foreign-currency long-term sovereign debt ratings over two years [TENTATIVE]

**Religious tensions amplify**: Religious tensions as a form of political instability exacerbate the climate risk premium — this is the paper's novel contribution to literature. [TENTATIVE]

**Financial development mitigates**: Well-developed financial markets reduce the negative fiscal effects of climate vulnerability by providing insurance, credit, and hedging instruments to firms and households, lessening the need for large government fiscal outlays.

### Methodology

Two-stage estimation:
1. **Local projections** (Jordà 2005) to estimate impulse responses of fiscal risk to climate vulnerability shocks
2. **Split-Panel Jackknife (SPJ)** bias correction to address Nickell bias inherent in dynamic panel models with fixed effects

The SPJ correction is the methodology component flagged for extraction (see `@concepts/split-panel-jackknife-spj.md`).

### Significance for Cemini

- **Macro strategy input**: Climate vulnerability scores can serve as leading indicators for sovereign bond spread widening in emerging-market portfolios
- **Religious tensions data**: Suggests incorporating religious/ethnic fractionalization indices into country risk models
- **Financial development as hedge**: Countries with deep financial markets are more resilient to climate shocks — relevant for country selection in macro strategies

### Caveats

- Single-source findings [TENTATIVE]; needs confirmation from independent datasets
- Data terminates in 2019 — pre-COVID, pre-energy-crisis. Radical data staleness risk flagged in evaluation brief
- Panel structure may mask country-specific heterogeneity
