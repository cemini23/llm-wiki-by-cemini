## Narrative

**[K23 DEEP-READ — 2026-05-01]** This paper introduces "Nomotic AI" as a conceptual category: the governance counterpart to agentic AI. It is a vocabulary-and-framework paper, not a technical implementation. The core argument: AI discourse has developed robust terminology for capability (agentic AI, tool use, multi-agent orchestration) but lacks equivalent vocabulary for governance. "Guardrails," "safety filters," and "responsible AI" are either too narrow, too vague, or too focused on harm prevention to capture the systematic governance architecture that agentic systems require. Nomotic AI fills that gap.

### Etymology and definition

Derived from Greek *nomos* (νόμος) — law, rule, custom, governance. In classical Greek thought, *nomos* was humanly constructed and deliberately established, as distinct from *physis* (nature). The etymology is deliberate: AI governance is a human responsibility. The rules don't emerge from the technology; they are constructed by the humans and organizations deploying it.

**Formal definition**: Nomotic AI is an intelligent governance layer for AI systems. It encompasses the rules, boundaries, authorities, and constraints that define what AI systems *should* do, as distinct from what they *can* do.

### The agentic-nomotic duality

| Dimension | Agentic AI | Nomotic AI |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Focus | Capability | Accountability |
| Core Question | What can this system do? | What should this system do? |
| Characteristic Verbs | Perceive, Reason, Plan, Act | Govern, Authorize, Trust, Evaluate |
| Function | Integration Layer | Governance Layer |
| Based On | Actions | Directives (Laws) |
| Risk When Absent | Inert governance structures | Ungoverned capabilities |
| Temporal Emphasis | Runtime execution | Pre-action authorization |

This is the paper's central framing device: action and law, capability and accountability, as a complementary pair. Neither is complete without the other. "Actions without laws are chaos. Laws without actions are inert."

### Intent vs. authority — the key distinction

The paper draws a sharp line between **intent** (which originates with users — it lives in the prompt, the instruction, the goal) and **authority** (which lives in the governance layer — it answers "is this action permitted?"). A user may intend for an AI system to perform an action the system is not authorized to perform. The nomotic layer governs whether the agentic layer may act. This reframes AI failures: most aren't failures of action but failures of authorization — the governance was inadequate, not the system.

### Six core principles

1. **Governance as Architecture** — Governance is an architectural decision, not a compliance layer. It must be designed in from the beginning. When governance is architectural, responsible behavior is the default. When retrofitted, it becomes friction.

2. **Pre-Action Authorization** — Governance must exist before action, not after. Post-hoc review is insufficient: by the time review occurs, the action is taken and harm is done. Every significant action must be evaluated against governance criteria before execution. The paper acknowledges that in high-speed systems this may operate through pre-authorized action classes or asynchronous monitoring rather than synchronous per-action approval.

3. **Explicit Authority Boundaries** — AI systems possess only the authority deliberately delegated to them. Implicit authority is dangerous. Authority in Nomotic AI is always delegated, never inherent. Actions outside explicit boundaries should fail or escalate, not proceed by default.

4. **Verifiable Trust** — Trust must be earned through evidence and verified through observation. It should not be assumed from capability. Trust is specific to particular actions, conditions, and contexts, and is monitored and adjusted based on observed behavior. Both over-trust and under-trust cause failures.

5. **Ethical Justification** — AI actions must be justifiable, not merely executable. Explainability is a component: if an action cannot be explained, it cannot be justified. Ethics is not a separate constraint but woven throughout all governance decisions.

6. **Accountable Governance** — AI systems cannot be accountable; they are tools. Accountability rests with humans who define rules, grant authorities, and set ethical standards. Every rule must have an owner; every authorization must trace to a responsible party.

### Positioning vs. related work

The paper explicitly distinguishes Nomotic AI from three adjacent concepts:

- **Constitutional AI** (Bai et al., 2022): Training-time approach that embeds behavioral constraints during model training. Nomotic AI operates post-training, at runtime. Constitutional AI shapes what the model *becomes*; Nomotic AI governs what deployed agents *do*.
- **Runtime policy enforcement engines**: Monitor inputs/outputs against defined policies in real time. Nomotic AI encompasses this but frames it within a broader architecture including authority delegation, trust verification, and ethical evaluation. Enforcement is one component, not the whole.
- **Delegated authority models** (e.g., GNAP RFC 9635): Scope permissions in multi-agent systems. Nomotic AI incorporates delegated authority as a core principle but extends beyond permissions to intent-vs-authority, verifiable trust over time, and ethical justification.

The paper also cites Deloitte 2025 (30% exploring agentic AI, only 14% ready to deploy), MIT Sloan 2026 (>1/3 of companies deploying agentic capabilities), and Gartner 2025 (predicting >40% of enterprise agentic AI projects canceled by 2027 due to governance gaps).

### Enforcement discussion

The paper addresses enforcement briefly but explicitly: in distributed multi-agent systems, the nomotic layer must have architectural authority to intervene — "kill switches, circuit breakers, or coordination protocols that can halt agent processes regardless of where they execute." It references OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026 (ASI02 tool misuse, ASI03 identity/privilege abuse). "Governance without enforcement capability is aspiration, not architecture."

### What the paper is NOT

The paper is explicit about scope limits: "This paper presents Nomotic AI as a conceptual framework, not a complete implementation guide. Significant work remains in developing specific architectures, testing governance approaches in production environments, and understanding the trade-offs between governance rigor and operational efficiency."

## Snippets

**S1 — Core definition** (p.5, §1):
> "Nomotic AI, derived from the Greek nomos meaning law, rule, or governance, represents the governance counterpart to agentic AI. Where agentic AI addresses capability, Nomotic AI addresses accountability. Where agentic AI enables action, Nomotic AI defines the laws under which action is permitted."

**S2 — The agentic-nomotic relationship** (p.12, §4):
> "Agentic AI is about actions. What systems can do. Nomotic AI is about laws. What systems should do. Actions without laws are chaos. An agentic system with no governance structure can do anything its capabilities permit. Laws without actions are inert. A governance framework with no corresponding capability produces nothing."

**S3 — Intent vs. authority** (p.13, §4):
> "Intent originates with users and programmers. It lives in the input, the prompt, the instruction, the goal provided to the system. The agentic system itself does not have intent. It has instructions. Authority determines whether the instruction should be executed. Authority lives in the governance layer. Intent and authority are distinct. A user may intend for an AI system to perform an action that the system is not authorized to perform. In such cases, proper governance prevents execution despite the presence of intent."

**S4 — Governance as Architecture** (p.4, §1):
> "Effective AI governance is not a checklist. It is an architectural decision. When governance is designed into AI systems from the beginning, outcomes change. Clear boundaries reduce ambiguity. Defined authority eliminates confusion about decision ownership. Explicit constraints mean edge cases are anticipated rather than discovered in production."

**S5 — Principle 1: Governance as Architecture** (p.16, §5):
> "Governance is an architectural decision, not a compliance layer. It must be designed into AI systems from the beginning, not added after deployment. When governance is treated as afterthought, it becomes friction. It fights against system behavior rather than shaping it. When governance is architectural, responsible behavior becomes the default."

**S6 — Principle 2: Pre-Action Authorization** (p.17, §5):
> "Governance must exist before action, not after. AI systems should verify authorization prior to execution, not seek forgiveness following failure. Post-hoc review is insufficient for AI governance. By the time a review occurs, the action has been taken, the outcome has occurred, and any harm has been done."

**S7 — Principle 3: Explicit Authority Boundaries** (p.18, §5):
> "Authority must be explicitly defined, not assumed. AI systems possess only the authority deliberately delegated to them. Implicit authority is dangerous. When boundaries are unclear, systems may take actions that no one intended to authorize. Authority in Nomotic AI is always delegated. AI systems have no inherent authority. They have only what humans grant them."

**S8 — Principle 4: Verifiable Trust** (pp.18–19, §5):
> "Trust must be earned through evidence and verified through observation. It should not be assumed from capability or claimed through assertion. Misplaced trust is a common source of AI failure. Trust calibration is essential. Over-trust leads to inadequate oversight and unexpected failures. Under-trust leads to excessive friction and unrealized value."

**S9 — Principle 5: Ethical Justification** (p.19, §5):
> "AI actions must be justifiable, not merely executable. The question of whether an action is right must be answerable. Capability to act does not imply appropriateness of action. Explainability is a component of ethical justification. If an action cannot be explained, it cannot be justified. If it cannot be justified, its appropriateness cannot be established."

**S10 — Principle 6: Accountable Governance** (pp.19–20, §5):
> "AI systems cannot be accountable. They are tools. Accountability rests with humans: those who define rules, grant authorities, establish trust parameters, and set ethical standards. For every rule in the nomotic framework, someone is responsible for its adequacy. For every authorization granted, someone is responsible for its appropriateness."

**S11 — Enforcement requirement** (p.20, §5):
> "Governance without enforcement capability is aspiration, not architecture. The nomotic layer must have architectural authority to intervene. This may require kill switches, circuit breakers, or coordination protocols that can halt agent processes regardless of where they execute."

**S12 — Nomotic AI vs. Constitutional AI** (pp.10–11, §3):
> "Constitutional AI embeds behavioral constraints during model training, using predefined principles to shape outputs. This is primarily a training-time approach. Nomotic AI operates post-training, providing governance for agentic systems at runtime. Constitutional AI shapes what the model becomes. Nomotic AI governs what deployed agents do."

**S13 — Conceptual contribution, not implementation** (p.24, §7):
> "The contribution of this paper is not a new enforcement mechanism or training technique. It is a conceptual reframing that makes governance visible, nameable, and architecturally co-equal with capability. If the term 'agentic AI' has shaped how practitioners think about what AI systems can do, 'Nomotic AI' offers equivalent vocabulary for what AI systems should do. That vocabulary has been missing. This paper provides it."
