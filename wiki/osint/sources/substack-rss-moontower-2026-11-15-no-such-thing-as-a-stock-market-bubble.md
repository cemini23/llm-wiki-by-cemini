## Narrative

A response to Jeff Yass's WSJ op-ed "There's No Such Thing as a Stock-Market Bubble". The argument under discussion: when share prices fall after a technology shock, that is uncertainty resolving, not a delusion correcting.

The mechanism the author adds. A stock price is a probability-weighted expected value under uncertainty. A sensible expected value and a most-likely outcome of zero can coexist, because expected value multiplies probability by outcome. Extrinsic value decays as forked paths close, faster than fundamentals can compensate — the same way a put can lose money while the stock falls.

The instrument that shows the shape. A stock price is a single number; an option surface across strikes and expiries shows which scenarios the market is pricing. The worked example is the natural-gas March-April spread ("widowmaker"): unbounded upside in a squeeze, but the highest probability mass sits near $3-4, so far out-of-the-money calls are expensive while deep-upside butterflies price near zero.

The practical read: high call premiums at extreme strikes are not evidence of an expected move. They price a low-probability tail.
