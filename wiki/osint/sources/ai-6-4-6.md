## Narrative

A legal-scholarship article arguing that AI law systematically fails to address "AI harms" — harms produced by AI operations that impair civil rights and democratic values. Lu's central observation is that these harms are typically *intangible, small, and invisible*: unlike a recognized physical injury, a single unauthorized data collection or a single algorithmic manipulation looks innocuous, yet AI repeats and aggregates them across millions to billions of people. Because they are diffuse and cumulative, victims lack knowledge of them, regulators lack foresight to investigate, legislators underestimate their gravity, and companies lack incentives to fix them.

The article's core contribution is a **legal typology of four primary AI harms**: eroding privacy, undermining autonomy, diminishing equality, and impairing safety. It identifies **two aggravating factors** — *accountability paucity* and *algorithmic opacity* — that let minor harms escalate by obstructing detection and correction. Lu then runs comparative case studies of three regulatory models: U.S. "legal consumerism" (consumer-centric, with an incompatible harm concept), EU "legal fundamentalism" (risk-based governance — the EU AI Act — with an incomplete harm concept), and Japanese "ethical activism" (soft-law governance with an ambiguous harm concept). All three are found insufficient: each overlooks certain harm types or ignores cumulative effects, letting problematic AI practices circumvent legal obligations. Drawing on this, Lu proposes a "harm-based turn" — recognizing AI harms *as harms, not mere risks* — operationalized through three interventions: **AI harm assessments**, **individual AI rights**, and **AI harm disclosures**, plus attention to normative tradeoffs.

**Cemini relevance**: the four-harm typology and two aggravating factors give Cemini a rigorous lens for self-assessing regulatory exposure; the U.S./EU/Japan comparative map is directly useful for scoping multi-jurisdiction compliance in institutional-acquirer due diligence, where opacity and accountability gaps are precisely what diligence teams probe.
