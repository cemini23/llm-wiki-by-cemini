## Narrative

Direct Air Carbon Capture and Storage (DACCS) removes CO2 from the air and stores it underground. Klement notes the technology is controversial because it is unclear whether it ever works at reasonable cost and scale. He also notes it may be the only way to abate emissions from cement production and similar processes.

The post summarises Bernecker and Müsgens (2026) of Brandenburg University of Technology. The study runs scenarios for capturing and storing 100 Mt of CO2 per year across Europe.

Six maps carry the result. The comparison is between three levels of integration:

1. **Technology in isolation.** The model picks optimal storage sites and CO2 pipelines without regard to the existing energy system or to where emissions occur.
2. **Added to the existing electricity network.** The optimal network accounts for electricity demand and the current grid.
3. **Fully integrated with power generation planning.** New power stations are planned with both conventional electricity demand and direct air capture in mind.

Each pair of maps also varies the storage geography. The left scenario allows storage only in the North Sea basin. The right scenario allows storage sites at suitable locations across Europe that have already been proposed.

The finding Klement highlights is that the network changes substantially with the level of integration. A plan built on technology in isolation is not the plan you get when generation capacity is co-optimised.

The investment relevance is capex timing. Which pipeline routes and storage sites survive depends on how the plan is scoped, so a site that looks optimal in isolation can drop out once the power system is included.

The underlying study is not read here. All figures are second-hand through Klement.
