## Narrative

### The study

Klement cites a survey of more than **9,000** US workers by Deming et al. (2026). The survey records time in meetings, meeting count, industry, rank, age, gender and education.

Findings:

- Managers spend far more time in meetings than non-managers.
- Post-pandemic, more meetings are online than in person.
- Men, older workers and college graduates spend more time in meetings. Klement argues this mostly reflects the manager gap.
- People in finance and IT hold more meetings per week than people in public administration.

### The cost estimate

The paper's headline estimate: in finance and IT, about **20%** of the wage bill pays for people attending a meeting. In retail the share is about **10%**.

That is a wage-cost accounting result, not a productivity result. It prices attendance, not output.

### Why it is filed here

The useful part is the measurement method. A meeting has a computable cost: attendee count times loaded hourly wage times duration. Any operator who runs recurring review rituals can apply it.

Klement's own anecdote sets the scale: he sometimes spent 30 to 40 hours a week in meetings.

No Cemini project needs this. It is filed as a management-cost reference and as a template for pricing recurring operator rituals such as the federation morning summary.
