## Narrative

### The setup

An earlier post covered an OECD study on start-up financing. Its finding: growth firms in Europe raise about the same as US peers on average, but future unicorns raise far more in the US.

This post adds a second study: Bo Becker, Efraim Benmelech and Joao Monteiro (2026). That study looks at listed companies only. Klement argues the same effect likely applies to private firms before listing.

### The finding

The original research question was why the largest megacaps are almost all American. Europe has no trillion-dollar company. The authors reject a fundamentals explanation. Instead, Europe has fewer new listings, the listings are worth less, and listed firms grow less after listing.

The answer they give is **cost of capital**.

Klement states his prior was the opposite. He expected a lower European cost of debt, because policy rates are lower. The Fernandez annual survey suggests cost of equity is roughly equal in the two regions. The data contradicts his prior: the cost of capital is **higher** for European firms.

### The size premium

Firms are ordered by market-value percentile. For firms with below-median market cap, the European cost-of-capital premium over the US is much larger than for above-median firms. The gap widened in the second half of the 2010s, which made it harder for European small caps to fund growth.

That is the load-bearing claim: the disadvantage is a **size premium**, not a level difference. A European small cap pays more for capital than a US small cap of the same size. [TENTATIVE: single newsletter, quoted study]

### Why it is filed here

It extends @concepts/european-startup-scaling-gap.md from the supply side to the price side. The two halves combine: Europe does not lack early capital, it prices late capital badly for small firms.

No Cemini lane trades European small caps. The note is macro context and a possible screen definition, not a position.
