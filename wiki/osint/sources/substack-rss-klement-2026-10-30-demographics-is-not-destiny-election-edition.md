## Narrative

Klement on Investing published this post on 2026-10-30. The author writes before the US midterm elections on the following Tuesday. He says he has no special insight into who will control Congress for the next two years, and he gives no forecast. He uses the vote to repeat his view that demographics is not destiny. He says demographic change is important, but it is so slow that many other factors dominate it.

He cites a New York Times opinion post by Thomas Edsall, which he bookmarked in the spring. Edsall claims that the Democratic Party has a good chance of winning the 2028 presidential election. Edsall also claims that demographic change in the United States will likely increase the Republican inbuilt advantage in the Electoral College from 2032 to 2040. The stated cause is the 2030 census. That census would remove Electoral College votes from states with declining populations, such as New York and California, and add votes to states with rising populations, such as Texas and Florida. The post calls New York and California reliably blue and Texas and Florida reliably red. On that basis, the reported Edsall analysis could shift some 12 votes in favour of Republicans until the 2040 census. The figure of some 12 votes is a single-source number in this newsletter. The file does not quote Edsall's original text.

The author says this analysis assumes that voters in the 2030s will vote on the same demographic lines as today. It assumes that black and highly educated voters vote reliably Democratic, and that older voters and working-class white people vote reliably Republican. He calls that a brave assumption. He says old people die, so the party choice of older voters in the 2030s is unknown. He says working-class white people were historically a key constituency for the Democrats. He says Hispanics currently tend to vote more Democratic than Republican. He says they are highly religious and may switch to the Republicans over time if Democratic policies alienate them.

He recommends a paper by Richard Calvo and collaborators. The paper tests how well election predictions based on demographic variables worked in the past. The charts show two tests. One test uses demographic trends at the last election to forecast the next election. The other test forecasts three elections ahead, the same horizon Edsall used. The dark black line is the demographic forecast. The light grey line is the actual election result. The author says the lines show no resemblance. He says demographic forecasts of election trends are a complete waste of time. The post credits the chart to Calvo et al. (2026). It gives no error statistic, vote margin, or sample size from that paper. The waste-of-time line is the author's reading of the charts.

For a quant-finance and macro research reader, the post is a claim about a slow input to political scenarios. Edsall maps state population change and current party lean into Electoral College votes from 2032 to 2040. The author says the same style of demographic forecast did not match later election results in the Calvo charts. The only magnitude in the post is "some 12 votes", and that number comes from one cited opinion post.
