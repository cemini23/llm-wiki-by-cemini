## Narrative

Canada's trade share with the US has fallen to the lowest level in more than 30 years. Both the US import share and the US export share dropped significantly since 2025.

Three forces drive the shift. Trump's trade war supplies the shock. Consumer boycotts supply the demand-side push. A formal Canadian government programme to boost non-US trade supplies the policy direction.

The scale of the move so far: non-US exports are up more than 40% over the last twelve months. Prime Minister Mark Carney set a goal to double non-US exports over the next decade. The article states he is already halfway to that goal.

America is still far and away the largest trading partner, at eight times the size of the second-place EU. The structural factors that made the relationship valuable also make deep decoupling painful. Canada has a smaller population and lower income than the US, so its whole economy is about the size of New York or Texas. Most major Canadian cities sit closer to American neighbours than to other Canadian cities. Almost every major province therefore trades more with the US than with the rest of Canada.

Business survey evidence from the Bank of Canada: more than 20% of firms plan to restructure supply chains or have done so. Another 10% are diversifying export markets. Firms reducing Canadian operations outnumber firms expanding them nearly two to one. Nearly 13% are cutting investment in Canada or moving it abroad.

Timing gives a natural experiment. The survey ran as Trump applied tariffs to another $20B of Canadian goods and threatened to double tariffs on Canadian vehicles at the start of 2027. Comparing before and after, the new tariffs and threats cut nearly 4 percentage points from export growth expectations, added half a percentage point to inflation expectations, and caused 25% of firms to revise down domestic investment.

The macro result so far is milder than the sentiment. Canadian GDP stagnated in 2025 and rose 1.4% in 2026. Real manufacturing output is up 3.5%, construction 1.8%, and energy 1.3%. The article concludes that decoupling has already blunted the tariffs and improved Canada's negotiating leverage. It also notes that the non-US export gain leans on rising energy and gold prices, which is an outside tailwind unlikely to repeat.

The figures are the author's, drawn from official statistics and the Bank of Canada survey. They are single-source here.
