## Narrative

A game-theoretic study of an underappreciated AI-governance failure mode: deploying AI in a competitive, pay-for-performance (PFP) workplace can *backfire*, lowering overall firm productivity rather than raising it. The mechanism: when a firm deploys AI to track high-performers' task actions and codify their know-how into recommendation systems and training, the AI acts as an involuntary conduit of knowledge transfer to lower-skilled employees. This "learning effect" helps low performers, but the resulting "competition effect" can flip the performance ranking between high-tangible/low-intangible-skill (HL) employees and low-tangible/high-intangible-skill (LH) employees — demotivating HL employees who lose their rank and effort incentive under PFP.

The authors model employees as competing on both tangible ("hard") and intangible ("soft") skills, where AI improves only tangible skills. Key findings: payoffs from AI deployment depend on workforce heterogeneity, reliance on tangible skills, the skill gap between employees, and AI efficacy. The central counterintuitive result (Proposition 5) is that **pursuing the most advanced AI is not optimal** — firms maximize profit by choosing an AI efficacy level "just enough to avoid the flipping" of performance ranking. Optimal AI efficacy rises with the tangible-skill gap and falls with the intangible-skill gap. Naive remedies — guaranteeing or raising wages of adversely affected employees, or strategic hiring of fewer high-tangible-skill workers — do not reliably fix the problem. The generalized model shows high-skill employees may even strategically withhold output to deny the AI training data.

**Cemini relevance**: a concrete reminder that "deploy the strongest model" is not a defensible governance posture; for institutional-acquirer due diligence, Cemini's AI-adoption story should show calibration of AI capability against workforce-incentive effects, not maximalism.
