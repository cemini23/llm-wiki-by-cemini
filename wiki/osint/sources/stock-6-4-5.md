## Narrative

Chok and Qian integrate two sociological perspectives — bounded rationality in stock markets and information inference from peer firms — to examine how the number of comparable publicly traded stocks affects IPO entry rates into NASDAQ. They hypothesize and find a curvilinear (inverted-U) relationship: more comparable stocks initially facilitate IPO entry (by providing information to infer valuations), but beyond a certain point, information overload discourages entry.

### Key findings

1. **Curvilinear relationship**: IPO entry rates increase with the number of comparable stocks up to a point, then decrease — too many comparables create information overload [Source: stock 6 4:5.pdf p.1].

2. **Mediation**: Trading volume and changes in stock returns partially mediate the relationship between comparable stocks and IPO entry rates [Source: stock 6 4:5.pdf p.1].

3. **Bounded rationality mechanism**: When stock markets are boundedly rational, it doesn't matter whether stock changes are positive or negative — both provide useful inference signals. But information processing capacity is finite, creating the curvilinear dynamic [Source: stock 6 4:5.pdf p.1-2].

4. **Density dependence**: The finding parallels organizational ecology's density-dependence theory (Haveman): increasing density initially provides legitimacy, then competition sets in [Source: stock 6 4:5.pdf p.2].

### Cemini relevance

**Low**. This is a niche economic-sociology paper on IPO entry dynamics. The curvilinear information-overload finding is conceptually relevant (too much comparable-company data can degrade, not improve, screening quality), but there's no directly actionable trading signal. Worth citing as a cautionary note on information overload in factor screening, not as a strategy input.
