## Narrative

The first federal-court order in this regulatory cycle to halt a state criminal prosecution of Kalshi on preemption grounds. The structural significance is not the TRO's duration (14 days) but its reasoning: a sitting federal district judge found the CFTC *"likely to ultimately win"* on the merits of federal preemption over state regulation of event contracts. That preliminary finding was the basis for rejecting *Younger* abstention — the doctrine that normally requires federal courts to defer to ongoing state criminal proceedings.

### Why *Younger* was rejected

*Younger v. Harris* abstention typically blocks federal-court interference in pending state criminal cases. Liburdi's order navigates around it by relying on the fact that *the United States itself is a party* to the federal action — not Kalshi alone. When the federal government sues a state, the comity concerns underlying *Younger* are inverted: the federal sovereign asserting preemption is not the kind of "interference" *Younger* was designed to prevent. This is a doctrinal opening that AG Mayes' brief presumably did not foresee, and it tracks the broader CFTC strategy that Selig confirmed on April 16 (`@sources/cftc-selig-house-ag-testimony-2026-04-16.md`) — *the federal government, not Kalshi alone, is the plaintiff in the AZ/CT/IL preemption suits*.

### TRO expiration and what comes next

The 14-day TRO expired April 24 2026 (yesterday from this brief's composition date). Standard Rule 65 procedure: TRO converts to preliminary-injunction proceedings, which require a fuller adversary hearing. If Liburdi extends or converts to a preliminary injunction, AZ's criminal case stays frozen pending merits adjudication. If he declines, the AZ criminal prosecution can resume — and the federal-vs-state preemption question moves to the merits docket on a faster timeline.

### Architectural significance for Cemini

Liburdi's preliminary preemption finding is one of three federal-court rulings (NJ Third Circuit upheld Kalshi's NJ injunction 2-1 ~Apr 6-9; Tennessee federal court ruled for Kalshi, per the Sixth Circuit split coverage) currently aligned with CFTC's exclusive-jurisdiction theory. The opposing data points are state-court rulings (NV TRO Mar 21, MA earlier wins) that hold sports-event contracts subject to state gambling licensing regardless of federal label. The Sixth Circuit split is the standard SCOTUS-cert trigger.

For the compliance-leverage thesis at `@entities/platforms/kalshi.md` role 3, this order is positive evidence — but contingent. The thesis strengthens if preemption holds at SCOTUS; it weakens to a state-by-state burden cascade if SCOTUS sides with states. See `@concepts/dcm-state-preemption.md` for the structural framing.
