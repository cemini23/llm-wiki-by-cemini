## Narrative

The article argues that publishing many low-value AI-generated pages can hurt a site's search visibility. The loss can be **sitewide**, not limited to the bad pages. Two traits drive the risk: the content is unoriginal, or it falls outside the site's area of expertise [Source: sterlingsky.ca/dangers-thin-scaled-ai-content/, retrieved 2026-11-25].

**The main example.** A personal injury law firm published one article for each local car crash reported in the news. Its organic rankings fell sharply. The site reportedly did not rank even for a branded search. The drop appears tied to the May 2026 core update [TENTATIVE].

**A second example that weakens the simple story.** Another law firm declined with a single page, updated from a police department's Facebook feed. Page count alone is not the cause.

**Google's definition.** Google calls this "scaled content abuse" and defines it as many pages generated mainly to manipulate rankings, not to help users.

**Why the topic was a mismatch.** People who search for a specific crash usually want news. Google shows news sources for those queries. A law firm writing "what to do after an accident" fits its expertise. Hundreds of crash summaries do not.

**What the article admits.** The author calls the pattern a pattern and says it does not prove causation. He also advises checking rank positions, not the estimated traffic that SEO tools report.

**Recommendations.**

1. Do not scale content that only repeats what already exists, such as local news reports.
2. Check topical fit first. Ask whether the audience would expect this content from this site.
3. Publish what relates directly to the services and expertise.
4. Do not build a strategy on testing how much low-value content Google will allow.
5. Judge algorithm updates by organic position trends, not by tool-based traffic estimates.

**What this adds to the wiki.** The wiki already treats thin content as a quality problem. This source adds three points: the risk is sitewide, the deciding test is topical fit rather than word count, and position data beats tool traffic estimates for judging the effect.

**Limit.** This is one agency's reading of two case patterns. There is no controlled measurement. Treat the mechanism as plausible and the numbers as absent.
